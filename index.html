Each digit is a T (−1), 0, or 1 (+1) — and negation is just flipping every digit.

Why a third state?

  • Symmetry. {−1, 0, +1} is symmetric around zero, so negative numbers need no sign bit and no two's-complement — you negate a number by flipping each digit.
  • Density. Base 3 is the integer base closest to e, the theoretical optimum — a trit carries ~1.58 bits, so fewer digits represent the same range.
  • History & future. The Soviet Setun (1958) was a real, working ternary computer; Knuth called balanced ternary “perhaps the prettiest number system of all.” And today, ternary weights {−1, 0, +1} are a live topic in efficient AI (BitNet b1.58).

What you can build

Native components for sources, gates (STI / PTI / NTI, MIN, MAX), a full-trit adder, MUX, registers, RAM/ROM, and an ALU — plus kits of gate-level subcircuits for arithmetic, sequencing, control, and neural nets. Define your own gates from a truth table, let the built-in minimizer reduce them, pack circuits into reusable blocks, watch timing & glitches, and share any circuit as a link.

How it works & what it models

TritLogic simulates logic at the idealised-trit level: every wire carries one of −1, 0, +1 (plus high-impedance Z and contention X for buses). It models gate behaviour, propagation delay, and dynamic hazards — but not continuous voltages, optical power, or analog noise. It's a tool for reasoning about ternary logic and architecture, in the spirit of Logisim for binary. An honest niche: it targets the ternary / multi-valued-logic curious — students, educators, and hobbyists — rather than claiming to be production EDA.
